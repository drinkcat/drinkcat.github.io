In this series I will show how you can use a cheap close-up filter to take macro pictures. This post is a general introduction, and the [next post will show you how to fix chromatic aberration](/blog/2013/03/18/macro-photography-correcting-chromatic-aberration/) introduced by the filter.

There are many ways of obtaining relatively large magnification with a camera. You can find out about many of these methods on the excellent [Micro 4/3rds Photography](http://m43photo.blogspot.com/) blog: using a [dedicated macro lens](http://m43photo.blogspot.no/2010/07/panasonic-leica-lumix-dg-macro-elmarit.html), [extension rings](http://m43photo.blogspot.no/2010/03/macro-rings.html), a [reverser ring](http://m43photo.blogspot.sg/2012/02/reverser-ring-for-macro.html), or a [close-up filter](http://m43photo.blogspot.sg/2012/09/using-10-macro-lens.html)...

I'm not willing to spend more than 500$ for a dedicated macro lens, so I went the cheapest possible way: using a [8x close-up filter](http://dx.com/p/premium-8x-macro-effect-camera-lens-filter-52mm-17323), that you can find for 8$ on dealextreme, and maybe cheaper on eBay. I went for the 8x filter instead of the 10x, because the latter has bad reviews: the lens element protrudes at the back of the filter, and you risk scratching your lens when mounting the filter (Fredrik Gløckner talks about it in [this post](http://m43photo.blogspot.sg/2012/09/using-10-macro-lens.html)); it also seems to be optically inferior.

{% include img.html src="/images/macro-photography-cheap/P1160771.JPG" alt="8$ close-up filter." %}

I tried the filter with 2 lenses: Panasonic 14-42mm, and 45-150mm. Both use a 52mm thread, so I could reuse the same filter. In each case, I use the camera in manual focus, set the focus to minimum distance, to obtain as much magnification as possible, and take a picture of a ruler.

### Magnification

The magnification is defined as the ratio between the sensor size and the object size. On a Micro 4/3, the sensor size is 17.3mm, so if you can image 17.3mm of a ruler, you have a 1:1 magnification. If you get 8.15mm, you have a 2:1 magnification, etc.

{% include img.html src="/images/macro-photography-cheap/P1160603.JPG" alt="Picture of a ruler. Here, you see about 15mm of the ruler, so the magnification is 17.3:15 = 1:0.87 or 1.15x. Note the chromatic aberrations on the side of the image, but more on that later..." %}

Clearly, only the 45-150mm lens at the maximum focal length, with the 8x filter, can be considered a true "macro" configuration. The rest does not provide enough magnification:

{% include img.html src="/images/macro-photography-cheap/montage.JPG" alt="Left to right: 45-150mm@150mm, 14-42mm@42mm, 14-42mm@14mm (always using the 8x filter). My Reuge/Romance Swiss-made music box does not seem to enjoy a lot the hot and humid weather or Singapore, leading to quite a thick layer of oxide on the metal..." %}

Interestingly, the 45-150mm, without filter, is a very bad lens for close-up works: the minimum focus distance is 0.9m, and leading to worse magnification than the 14-42mm. On the other hand, the 8x close-up filter has a huge effect on this lens. Why? No precise idea, but it has something to do with the lens design.

### Sample pictures

These sample pictures are taken with the 45-150mm lens, at 150mm, using a very small aperture (f/22), to get as much depth of field as possible. Sure, you will lose quite a bit a resolution due to diffraction, but I think this still gives best results, and makes focusing a little easier.

Macro photography needs external lighting, even on a sunny day you will not get enough light on the sensor. For this purpose, I use an external flash (an old [Nikon SB-22](http://www.kenrockwell.com/nikon/sb22.htm)), which I hold at a certain distance using a [TTL flash extension cord](http://www.ebay.com/itm/271118376948). The flash is unfortunately not fully compatible with Panasonic cameras (no TTL mode), so it sometimes takes a few tries to get the correct amount of light on the scene.

There is some obvious [chromatic aberration](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromatic_aberration) (red and blue fringes) on the sides of the image. I will explain the [method to correct chromatic aberration in the next post](/blog/2013/03/18/macro-photography-correcting-chromatic-aberration/).

The effect of the correction is quite striking on a 100% crop:

{% include img.html src="/images/macro-photography-cheap/montage-chroma.JPG" alt="Left: Original image. Right: Chromatic aberration corrected image." %}

Even after correction, the image still looks fairly blurry, possibly due to diffraction.

Finally, the corrected image looks very decent, especially considering the cost of the close-up filter.
